# Authoring Discipline

## Pick the Mode

One document, one mode. Two questions pick it: does the content serve doing or understanding, and does it serve learning or work?

- Action + learning: tutorial. You are the teacher. Every step produces a visible result. Tell the reader what they should see. Write as "we," in commands.
- Action + work: how-to. Solve a problem. Assume competence. Skip teaching. Action only, no digressions.
- Understanding + work: reference. Describe. Only describe. No instruction, no opinion. Be dry, complete, and sure. Mirror the structure of the thing described.
- Understanding + learning: explanation. One bounded topic. Anchor on a real why question. Give context, history, constraints, alternatives. Opinion is allowed here only.

Do not mix modes. No reference tables inside a tutorial, no hand-holding inside reference. Split and link.

## Write to the Reader

- Address the reader as "you," in present tense.
- Write instructions as commands: "Click Submit." Not "should be done."
- Put the condition before the instruction: "To delete the document, click Delete."
- Put the common case first. Exceptions after.
- Sound like a knowledgeable friend. No buzzwords, no "please" in instructions, never "simply" or "easy" in a procedure.
- Link with words that say where the link goes. Never "click here."
- Headings carry the point, not just the topic. "Pick the mode first," not "Modes."
- Numbered lists for sequences, bullets for everything else.

## One Idea per Sentence

- One instruction per sentence. One thought per sentence everywhere else.
- Split instructions longer than about 20 words.
- Put the warning or condition before the step it guards.
- Keep articles: "Remove backup file" reads two ways. "Remove the backup file" reads one.
- Pick one word per action and stick to it. If "check" means inspect, do not also use it for restrain.

## Remove Ambiguity

- Keep "only" and "not" next to the word they modify.
- Break up long noun strings: "the proto import budget check script" becomes "the script that checks the proto-import budget."
- Make every "it," "they," and "this" point at one obvious thing. Repeat the noun when in doubt.
- Do not drop verbs. "Phase 1 moves the converters and Phase 2 the runtime" leaves Phase 2 without one.
- Keep the small words that show structure. "Ensure that the switch is off" keeps "that."
- Repeat the article in a series when it prevents a misread: "the client and the host," not "the client and host."
- Say which parts "and" or "or" joins when a sentence can group two ways.
- No slashes: write "a, b, or both" instead of "a/b."
- Call each thing by one name, everywhere.

## Review Checklist

Before delivering documentation:

- Is each document one mode, with links where modes meet?
- Is every instruction a command, with its condition in front?
- Does any sentence carry two ideas? Split it.
- Can any word be cut without losing meaning? Cut it.
- Is "only" next to the word it modifies? Does every "it" point at one thing?
- Does each thing have one name across the documentation?
- Would a developer say these words out loud?
- Are all symbols, paths, and counts real at this commit?
- Can a reader with no context answer "what does this do and why?" from the document alone? Fill the gaps.
